Twelve photos, no labels. You decide which are undercooked, which are perfect, and which are burnt. Then the page trains a tiny classifier on your answers and you can feed it a new croissant to judge.

It never learns what a croissant looks like. It only measures three numbers about the colour of the crust — so whatever you teach it, it believes.

How does it actually decide? (plain language)

1. Measure. Each photo is shrunk to a small grid, and the middle of it — where the croissant sits — is scanned pixel by pixel. Pixels that are bright and colourless are treated as backdrop and skipped, so a cut-out photo on a white studio background is not mistaken for a pale croissant. Two numbers come out of what is left:

  • Warmth — how much redder than bluer the crust is. Raw dough is nearly neutral; a golden croissant is strongly red-over-blue. Turning the lights up scales red and blue together, so this number barely moves between a dim bakery and a bright studio.
  • Char — the share of pixels dark enough to read as burnt.
  • Brightness — the plain average lightness. This one is useful but least trustworthy, because it also records how bright the room was, so it is given half the say of the other two.

The chart can only draw two of the three, so it shows a flat slice through the third. The caption on the chart tells you which brightness the slice was cut at — move the brightness dial and the whole map shifts.

2. Learn. You label the twelve. For each label you used, the page averages those photos' three numbers into one "typical" point.

3. Decide. A new photo is measured the same way, and it gets whichever typical point is nearest. The confidence bars are just how much nearer the winner was.

What it is not doing: it never learns what a croissant looks like. Shape, layers, and crumb are invisible to it. Three numbers, your twelve examples, nearest match. That is all.

Three experiments worth trying
  1. Teach it something wrong. Label the twelve backwards — call the pale ones burnt and the dark ones undercooked. Train again. It will cheerfully agree with you, because it has no idea what the words mean.
  2. A close call. Use the dials. Slide crust warmth slowly and watch the verdict flip. Stop right on the flip: the top two scores should be nearly equal, which is the classifier admitting it is guessing.
  3. A strange one. Upload something that is not a croissant — a dark jacket, a bright wall, your desk. It still answers, but if the numbers land far outside your twelve photos it now warns you not to trust the answer.
